Woodworking is negotiation with material that remembers how it grew.

Every board carries grain, knots, tension, and hidden weakness. The worker can cut across these structures, but cannot pretend they are absent. Force applied in the wrong direction becomes a split. Patience applied along the grain becomes form.

The material is no longer a tree, but the tree has not entirely left it.

Those who work with wood learn to measure before removal. Once cut, length does not return. Once carved, the missing material becomes part of the design. Accuracy matters because correction usually means taking away more.

The patch respects this restraint.

Roots also create structure through direction. They branch where resistance allows and thicken where pressure repeats. Corruption resembles rot inside a beam: sometimes visible at the surface, sometimes discovered only after weight is applied.

A strong object does not depend on appearance alone.

Joints must hold when unseen. Supports must transfer force without announcing themselves. The best work often hides the complexity required to keep it standing.

Woodworkers understand that useful things can be repaired, but not indefinitely.

Eventually, a piece must be replaced, burned, or returned to the soil.

Until then, the hands continue shaping what remains, following the lines left by a life that grew before the tool arrived.
