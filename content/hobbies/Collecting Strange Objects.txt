Collecting strange objects begins when ordinary value stops being sufficient.

The collector notices what others discard: a key without a lock, a labelled fragment from an unknown machine, a stone shaped too precisely, a photograph containing someone no one remembers. Each object is incomplete, and incompleteness creates gravity.

The collection grows through unanswered questions.

Items are arranged by shape, origin, material, or a system understood only by the collector. Connections appear. Two unrelated objects begin looking like evidence of the same missing event. A pattern forms because the objects were gathered, but the collector may come to believe the pattern existed first.

The patch produces many strange objects.

Broken characters. Burned fragments. obsolete roots. Images of potatoes at corruption states no one recorded. Some should probably be deleted. Others feel dangerous to lose.

Collectors preserve uncertainty by giving it a shelf.

At low corruption, this protects overlooked history and creates unexpected meaning. At high corruption, possession becomes interpretation. The collector may believe each object arrived deliberately or that removing one would interrupt a larger mechanism.

Nothing in the collection is useless.

That is the rule.

The danger is that usefulness may be assigned after the fact.

Somewhere among the shelves is an object the collector does not remember finding. It has already been given a place.
