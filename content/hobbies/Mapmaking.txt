Mapmaking is the conversion of movement into symbols.

A path becomes a line. A boundary becomes a colour. Elevation becomes distance between marks. The map removes most of reality so the remaining pieces can guide someone through it.

Every map is useful because it is incomplete.

The maker decides what deserves representation. Roads may matter more than roots. Names may matter more than terrain. A place without a label can disappear from the traveller’s expectations even while remaining physically present.

The patch resists stable maps.

Corruption changes routes. Pages link in loops. Roots cross beneath visible structures. A potato may occupy the same coordinates while becoming functionally unreachable through altered connections.

Mapmakers respond by revising.

At low corruption, revision keeps the map alive. At high corruption, the document becomes a record of continuous failure. New lines cover old ones. Warnings multiply. The legend grows larger than the territory.

Still, the map offers a promise: that location can become understandable.

The danger is confusing the symbol with the place.

A route marked safe may no longer exist. An unmapped path may be the only one still open.

Somewhere near the edge, the cartographer draws a small mark without explanation.

Later, every route begins curving toward it.
