Metal detecting is the search for hidden objects through invisible response.

The ground appears empty until the device produces a tone. The signal does not reveal the object directly. It indicates conductivity, depth, shape, and uncertainty. The detectorist must decide whether the sound is worth disturbing the soil.

Most signals are ordinary.

Nails. Foil. Wire. Fragments of machines that no longer exist. Occasionally, the ground returns a coin, ring, tool, or object whose significance exceeds its material.

The patch contains many buried signals.

Some belong to previous states. Some were discarded deliberately. Some may have entered before the current system began recording events.

Metal detecting teaches selective attention.

Sensitivity set too low misses valuable objects. Set too high, every mineral becomes an alarm. Corruption behaves similarly. Increased awareness can reveal hidden patterns or overwhelm the observer with false positives.

The act of digging creates responsibility.

A hole must be opened carefully, the object removed, and the surface restored. Discovery should not leave unnecessary damage.

Not every signal deserves excavation.

Some buried things remain stable only while buried.

The detector sounds again.

The tone is strong, clean, and deeper than expected.

Beneath it, another identical signal answers from several metres away.
